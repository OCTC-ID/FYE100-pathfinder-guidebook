Lesson 1.4

Navigating AI Policies

Every course sets its own AI rules. Here's how to find them, and a routine for using AI well.

About 6 minutes 4 of 8 in this module

There's no single AI rule for college. Each course, and sometimes each assignment, sets its own.

In one course, you might be encouraged to brainstorm with AI. In another, using AI at all could count as an academic integrity violation. Both can be true in the same week. This lesson shows you where to find the rules, what to do when they aren't clear, and a simple routine to use any time you work with AI.

Where to find the rules

Look in two places, in this order.

  1. The AI Course Policy

    Look for the AI Course Policy section of your syllabus. It explains your instructor's overall approach to AI for that course.

  2. The assignment instructions

    Each assignment is labeled with a level of AI use. The level tells you how AI can be used on that specific piece of work.

See also

Lesson 1.1 on reading your syllabus like a contract.

Three levels of AI use

Assignments use one of three levels. Think of them as points on one scale, from no AI at all to AI as part of the assignment itself.

No AI

You do this work on your own. Your instructor chose this because the skill itself is what you're practicing.

Example: a reflection on your own goals, written entirely in your own words.

AI-supported

You can use AI for the parts your instructor names, like brainstorming or checking grammar. The thinking and the final work are yours.

Example: using Copilot to brainstorm topic ideas, then choosing one and writing the paper yourself.

AI-integrated

Using AI well is part of the assignment. You might check what AI gives you against a standard, or explain how you used it.

Example: asking AI a question, checking its answer against your textbook, and explaining what it got right and wrong.

Levels are chosen assignment by assignment, not for a whole course. The same course can have one assignment at No AI and the next at AI-integrated. Check the assignment instructions every time.

If an assignment doesn't list a level, or you aren't sure what it means, don't guess. Ask.

When the rules aren't clear

Ask before you use AI, not after. An instructor can easily answer a question before you start. It's much harder to sort things out once work has been turned in.

A specific question gets a specific answer. It also shows your instructor that you take the rules seriously.

The 3 Qs

Once you know the rules, three questions keep you on track. Each one belongs to a moment in your work.

  1. Before you ask

    Should I use AI for this part of my assignment?

    BE Responsible · BE Honest

  2. Before you use it

    How would I know if AI's answer is wrong?

    BE Critical

  3. Before you hand it in

    Can I explain my work and how AI helped me?

    BE Honest · BE Reflective

Notice the behaviors under each question. The 3 Qs are the five behaviors from Lesson 1.3 turned into a routine. The fifth, BE Safe, applies every time: never paste other people's personal information, passwords, or anything private into an AI tool.

Pathfinder Tip

When AI is optional, choosing not to use it is a good choice too.

Keep a trail

A trail is a record of how your work grew over time. It helps you explain how AI helped you, and it protects you if anyone ever has questions about your work.

  • Write in Word, saved to OneDrive. Word keeps a dated history of your draft as you write. Type your work in the document instead of pasting it in at the end.
  • Keep a OneNote notebook for each course. Save your notes, sources, and ideas there while you work.
  • Write down how you use AI. Copy your question into OneNote and note what you kept, changed, or left out.
