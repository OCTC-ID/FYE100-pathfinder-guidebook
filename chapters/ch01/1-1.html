Lesson 1.1

College-Level Expectations

The rules changed. Here's what nobody told you.

About 7 minutes 1 of 8 in this module

Most students who struggle in their first year of college aren't struggling because they aren't smart enough. They're struggling because the rules changed, and nobody told them how, or how much.

High school and college are both called "school." They look similar from the outside. But how they work, what they expect from you, and what happens when you fall short are fundamentally different. This lesson makes those differences visible so you can navigate them instead of running into them the hard way.

No matter where you're coming from (straight out of high school, a dual enrollment program, years in the workforce, or somewhere in between), this is worth your full attention.

Who's in this room

OCTC classrooms, online and in person, bring together students from very different places. Two of the most common are students coming from high school and students returning to education after time in the workforce. The expectations in this lesson apply to everyone, but they land differently depending on where you're starting.

If you're coming from high school

You may have been an excellent student. You may have earned high grades, taken advanced courses, and had teachers who knew your name and followed up when you were struggling.

College is different. The support is still here, but you have to seek it. Nobody is going to chase you down for a missing assignment. The expectation is that you're an adult managing your own academic life, even if you're 16 or 17.

The transition isn't about whether you're capable. Capability alone isn't enough; you also need to follow through, follow directions, and take ownership.

If you're returning after time away

You may have been out of school for years, maybe decades. You've been managing real responsibilities: jobs, families, finances, communities. That experience is not a disadvantage.

The professional habits you've built (showing up, communicating clearly, following through on commitments) are exactly what college expects. You already know more about accountability than you might think.

The adjustment is often the systems: Blackboard, academic language, citation formats, the pace of a semester. Those are learnable. The foundation you're bringing is solid.

The big shift: responsibility moves to you

In high school, the system was designed to catch you. In college, the system is designed to support you, but only if you engage with it.

This is the most important difference, and it shows up everywhere. In high school, teachers often remind you about deadlines, follow up when you're absent, and give you chances to redo work. In college, those things still exist, but they're your job to start, not your instructor's job to offer.

  • BeforeYour teacher reminded you about the assignment due Friday. If you missed it, they asked you what happened.
    NowThe due date is in the syllabus. If you're going to miss it, you reach out first, before the deadline, not after.
  • BeforeIf you didn't understand something, your teacher noticed and checked in with you.
    NowIf you don't understand something, you go to office hours, email your instructor, or visit the Teaching & Learning Center. You start the conversation.
  • BeforeYou could skim the directions and still do well because you had a sense of what was expected.
    NowDirections are specific and intentional. Not following them, even on a strong piece of work, costs you points. Read everything.
  • BeforeYour relationship with your teacher was ongoing. They knew you over time and gave you the benefit of the doubt.
    NowYour instructor may have 30, 60, or 150 students. They'll learn who you are through how you show up: your work, your communication, your follow-through.

The syllabus is a contract. Read it that way.

Every course you take has a syllabus. Most students skim it once and never look at it again. That's one of the most common mistakes in the first year of college.

Your syllabus tells you exactly what's expected, how you'll be graded, what happens if you miss a deadline, how to contact your instructor, and what the academic integrity policy is for that course. If something goes wrong and you say you didn't know, the first question will be whether it was in the syllabus.

  • Read it before the first week is over. Not just the assignment schedule; read the policies section too.
  • Note every deadline. Put them in your phone, your planner, or your weekly map. Don't trust yourself to remember.
  • Find the late work policy. Some instructors accept late work with a penalty. Some don't. Know which situation you're in before it matters.
  • Find the communication policy. How does your instructor want to be contacted? How quickly do they respond? Do they hold office hours?
  • Find the AI policy. It matters, and it varies from course to course.
Key term

Syllabus: the document that lays out a course's expectations, grading, deadlines, and policies. Each course has its own.

Pathfinder Tip

When something in the syllabus is unclear, ask before you act on your assumption. "I read the syllabus and wasn't sure how X works. Can you help me understand?" is a professional way to ask, and it shows you actually read it.

What instructors expect (that they don't always say out loud)

Four expectations come up in almost every college course. None of them are complicated. All of them are easy to miss if nobody points them out.

Follow the directions, all of them

Assignment directions exist for a reason. Word counts, formatting requirements, citation styles, submission formats: these aren't arbitrary. They reflect what the instructor is actually assessing. Submitting excellent content without following the directions tells your instructor you either didn't read them or decided they didn't apply to you. Neither is a good look.

This is one of the most common places strong students trip up in their first semester. The work is good, but points are lost for something entirely preventable.

Pathfinder Tip

Before you submit anything, reread the directions one more time and check your work against each stated requirement. Not "does this seem right," but "does this meet every requirement?"

Communicate like a professional

Email to your instructor is professional communication, not a text message. That means a subject line, a greeting, complete sentences, and a signature. It also means being specific about what you need instead of saying something is confusing.

The second version tells your instructor exactly what you need, shows you've already tried to figure it out, and treats them as a professional. That's the standard.

See also

Module 2 goes deeper on professional email.

Manage your own deadlines

Deadlines in college are real. Life happens, and most instructors are human about genuine emergencies. But "I forgot" or "I didn't realize it was due" isn't an emergency. It's a planning problem, and it's yours to own.

If something comes up that affects your ability to meet a deadline, reach out to your instructor before the deadline, not after. Most instructors are far more willing to work with a student who communicated early than one who disappeared and came back with an explanation after the fact.

Pathfinder Tip

An email the day before a deadline explaining a problem shows ownership. An email the day after asking for an exception asks your instructor to absorb the cost of your planning.

Academic integrity is non-negotiable

Academic integrity means your work represents your own thinking, effort, and understanding. It means citing sources when you use someone else's ideas. It means not submitting work that someone else, or an AI, produced as if it were your own. It means being honest about what you did and didn't do.

The consequences for academic integrity violations in college are real and serious, and they follow you. Take that seriously before you make a decision you can't undo.

Every course has an academic integrity policy, and many also have a specific AI policy. Read both. When you're unsure whether something crosses a line, ask your instructor before you do it, not after.

Key term

Academic integrity: doing your own work, giving credit to others' ideas, and being honest about how your work was produced.

Owning your own learning

Everything in this lesson comes back to one thing: in college, you are the person most responsible for your academic success. Not your instructor. Not your advisor. Not your family. You.

That's not a burden. It's an invitation. You have more say over your college experience than you've ever had over your education. You choose how much effort you put in, how much support you seek, and what you take away from each course. Nobody can do that for you.

The students who do well in college aren't necessarily the ones who were best prepared coming in. They're the ones who figured out how college works, and then worked it. This lesson is a head start on that.

Reflect

Look back at the four "Before / Now" shifts. Which one will take the most adjustment for you, and what's one thing you could do this week to get ahead of it?

You don't have to have all of this figured out on day one. Nobody does. But start paying attention to it now, because the habits you build in your first few weeks tend to stick.

Build good ones.
