Trail Tip

Wait... How Is My Grade Actually Calculated?

Most students don't know. Now you will.

About 8 minutes 6 of 8 in this module

At some point, you're going to check your grade and feel confused. The number won't match what you expected.

You'll wonder what's being counted, how much each assignment matters, and whether you can still turn things around. This Trail Tip answers those questions before you need them. There are two things to understand: how your grade works inside a single class, and how that grade feeds into your overall GPA. Both are simpler than they look, once someone explains them.

How your grade works in a class

Most courses use one of two grading approaches. Knowing which one your instructor uses changes how you think about every assignment.

Points-based grading

Every assignment is worth a set number of points. Your grade is your total earned points divided by the total possible points. That's it.

Points-based example
AssignmentEarnedPossible
Reflection 144
Reflection 234
Discussion11
Quick check0.51
Total8.510

8.5 ÷ 10 = 85%, a B

Weighted grading

Different categories of work count for different percentages of your grade. How you do in each category matters more than the points on any single assignment.

Weighted example
CategoryWeightYour scoreCounts as
Exams50%78%39.0
Assignments30%92%27.6
Participation20%85%17.0
Final grade83.6%

39.0 + 27.6 + 17.0 = 83.6%, a B

In weighted grading, a strong exam score matters more than a perfect homework record.

Which one does your course use?

Check each course's syllabus. It tells you whether the course is points-based or weighted, and how the work breaks down. Your syllabus is the contract for the course, so it's the place to look first, including for this one.

Don't fall into this trap

It's easy to look at a small assignment and think, "That's basically nothing." But small assignments add up. A handful of low-point activities can make up a real share of your final grade, and skipping them is one of the most common ways a B turns into a C. There are no throwaway assignments.

How your class grade feeds your GPA

Your GPA, or grade point average, summarizes your academic performance across all your courses. It isn't magic. It's math.

Key Term

Quality points: a course's grade points multiplied by its credit hours.

1Know the grade scale

Letter grades convert to grade points on a 4.0 scale.

  • A4.0
  • B3.0
  • C2.0
  • D1.0
  • E0.0

At KCTCS colleges, the failing grade is an E, not an F. An E earns no credit and 0 grade points, and it still counts in your GPA.

A D isn't always enough. A D earns credit at KCTCS, but some courses require a C or better to pass or to count toward your program. FYE 100 is one of them: you need at least a C to pass. Check each syllabus, and ask your Success Coach which courses in your program have a C-or-better rule.

Good to know

You may see other grades on your record. A W (withdrawal), I (incomplete), or P (pass in a pass-fail course) doesn't count in your GPA. In a pass-fail course, F means you didn't pass. See the KCTCS catalog (opens in a new tab) for the full list.

2Weigh each grade by its credit hours

Each course counts toward your GPA based on its credit hours. A 4-credit course has more impact than a 1-credit course, even if you earn the same grade in both. Multiply each course's grade points by its credit hours to get its quality points. Then divide the total quality points by the total credit hours.

GPA example
CourseCreditsGradeGrade pointsQuality points
ENG 1013A4.012.0
MAT 1503B3.09.0
BIO 1124C2.08.0
FYE 1001A4.04.0
Totals1133.0

GPA = 33 ÷ 11 = 3.0

Pathfinder Tip

Credit hours matter as much as grades. A C in a 4-credit course pulls your GPA down more than a C in a 1-credit course. When you're deciding where to put your study time, heavier courses deserve more of it.

Look back at

Lesson 5.1: Quantitative Reasoning. This is that skill, applied to your grades.

Try it

Work each problem on your own first, on paper or in your head. Then open the answer to check yourself.

Problem 1: Points-based

You've earned 76 out of 100 possible points in a course so far. What's your grade percentage, and what letter grade is that?

Show the answer

76 ÷ 100 = 76%, a C on a standard scale. Check your syllabus, since grade cutoffs can vary by course.

Problem 2: GPA

You take HIS 101 (3 credits) and earn a B, PSY 110 (3 credits) and earn an A, and MAT 110 (4 credits) and earn a C. What's your GPA?

Show the answer

Quality points: 3 × 3.0 = 9, 3 × 4.0 = 12, 4 × 2.0 = 8. Total 29 quality points ÷ 10 credits = 2.9.

Problem 3: Weighted

A course weights exams at 60% and assignments at 40%. You score 70% on exams and 95% on assignments. What's your final grade?

Show the answer

0.60 × 70 = 42, and 0.40 × 95 = 38. 42 + 38 = 80%, a B. Notice how the exam score held the grade down, even with strong assignments.

Problem 4: What if?

You have a 2.5 GPA after 20 credit hours. Next semester you take 12 credits and earn straight A's. Why does a higher-credit semester have more impact on your GPA than a lighter one?

Show the answer

Your old quality points are 2.5 × 20 = 50. The new semester adds 4.0 × 12 = 48. Your new GPA is 98 ÷ 32, about 3.06. More credit hours means more quality points going into the average, so a heavier semester moves your GPA further, up or down.

You don't have to wait until midterm to find out where you stand. Check your grades in Blackboard, read each syllabus, and do the math. If the numbers don't make sense, ask your instructor. That's what they're there for.
