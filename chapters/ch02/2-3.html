Lesson 2.3

Help-Seeking as a Strength

Asking for help is a skill successful students use every day.

About 6 minutes 3 of 8 in this module

College can feel like a long trail with steep climbs and unexpected turns, but no one reaches the summit alone.

Successful students know when and how to ask for help. That's a sign of confidence and self-awareness, not weakness. Reaching out early keeps small challenges from becoming big obstacles.

The students who struggle most aren't the ones who don't know things. They're the ones who wait too long to ask.

What help-seeking really means

It's more than raising your hand in class. Help-seeking means using your resources, relationships, and communication skills to stay on track before things go sideways. It can look like:

  • Asking a classmate to study together.
  • Emailing your instructor when you're confused about an assignment.
  • Meeting with your Success Coach to talk about goals or time management.
  • Visiting tutoring or the library for extra support on a specific skill.
  • Reaching out to financial aid or counseling when life gets complicated.

Each of these takes initiative. Initiative is the opposite of giving up.

Key term

Help-seeking: reaching out to people and resources on purpose, before a small problem becomes a big one.

Four tips for confident help-seeking

  1. Ask early

    Don't wait until you're overwhelmed. A question early in the semester is easier to answer than a crisis near the end.

  2. Be specific

    Instead of "I don't get this," try "I'm confused about this part. Could you walk me through another example?" Specific questions get specific help.

  3. Communicate professionally

    Be polite, be clear, and sign your name. Your instructors and advisors are colleagues, not customer service.

  4. Follow through

    Use the advice you get. If someone helps you, let them know how it went. That's how professional relationships are built.

Pay attention to where you are

These resources exist because OCTC knows that success isn't just about what happens in the classroom. They're here because students need them, and using them is one of the smartest moves you can make.

You don't have to be in crisis to ask for help. You just have to be paying attention to where you are on the trail.

Reflect

Think of a time when you asked for help, in school, at work, or in life, and it made things better. What gave you the courage to reach out? What happened as a result?
