Lesson 5.3

Where Does My Money Go?

You can't manage what you can't see. Awareness comes first.

About 10 minutes 5 of 7 in this module

Most people think they know where their money goes, until they actually look.

College makes spending even harder to track: gas, groceries, random fees, subscriptions you forgot you had, late-night delivery, "just one thing" from the store. It adds up faster than it should, and usually in ways you didn't see coming.

This lesson isn't about judging your spending. It's about seeing it clearly, and then giving your money a plan. Clarity is the real starting point for any financial decision, at any income, in any life situation.

You don't need to fix everything. You just need to see it first.

Why this matters, for everyone

Money stress shows up differently depending on where you're starting.

  • I live with my parents and have some money from work, but I never know where it goes by the end of the month.
  • I'm working full-time, paying rent, and trying to make it all stretch.
  • I have financial aid money right now, but I know it won't last the whole term.
  • I'm supporting my family. There's not much wiggle room, and I'm stressed about it constantly.

All four of those students could be in this course. All four benefit from the same first move: knowing what's actually happening with their money before trying to change it.

Three steps to seeing clearly

1Look at the last week or two

You don't need bank statements, though you can use them if you have them. Start by asking: where did my money go in the last week or two? Rough estimates are fine. You want a general picture, not an audit.

Common student spending categories:

  • Food: groceries, snacks, eating out, delivery
  • Transportation: gas, rideshare, parking
  • School: textbooks, supplies, fees, printing
  • Subscriptions: streaming, music, apps, cloud storage
  • Personal: toiletries, clothing, household
  • Social: going out, gifts, splitting bills
  • Bills: phone, rent, utilities, insurance
  • Random: convenience stores, online orders, impulse buys
Pathfinder Tip

Use the Dollar Tracker to make this concrete. Option A looks back at the past week. Option B tracks the next week as it happens. Pick the one that fits where you are.

2Notice what surprises you

Most people find at least one thing they didn't expect. That surprise is the point. It's where awareness starts doing its work.

  • "I eat out way more than I thought."
  • "Subscriptions are quietly taking more than I realized."
  • "Small purchases, $5 here and $8 there, add up to a lot."
  • "I didn't plan for school fees until they hit."
  • "I underestimated what gas actually costs me each week."
  • "There's a lot of 'I don't even remember buying that.'"

You don't have to fix anything yet. Just notice. That noticing is worth more than it sounds.

3Find your money leaks

A money leak is spending that doesn't line up with what you actually value or need. Not spending someone else thinks is unnecessary, but spending you recognize as out of line with your own priorities.

  • Delivery fees when you already have food at home
  • Subscriptions you forgot about and don't use
  • Convenience buys that happen because you didn't plan ahead
  • Impulse buys you regret within a day or two
  • Social spending that felt like an obligation instead of a choice
Pathfinder Tip

A money leak isn't the same as spending on something you enjoy. If eating out truly matters to you, that's a priority, not a leak. The leak is spending that leaves you wondering where your money went.

Give every dollar a job

Once you know where your money goes, you can decide where it should go. The approach that works best for most students is a zero-based budget: when money comes in, you give every dollar a job until there are zero dollars left without one.

Zero doesn't mean you spend it all. Savings is a job. Paying down debt is a job. Fun is a job too. The difference is that you decide on purpose, when the money arrives, instead of trying to figure out at the end of the month what happened.

Key Term

Zero-based budget: a plan that gives every dollar coming in a job (spending, saving, or paying debt) until money in minus money assigned equals zero.

  1. List what's coming in this month

    Paychecks, financial aid, help from family, anything else. If your income changes, plan on the lowest amount you can count on. If a financial aid refund has to last the whole term, divide it across the months it needs to cover.

  2. List every place it needs to go

    Start with your Musts: housing, food, getting to school and work, bills. Then the rest: savings, school costs, personal, fun.

  3. Assign until you reach $0

    Give each category an amount until money in minus money assigned equals zero. If you run out before you run out of categories, trim something. If money is left over, give it a job, even if the job is savings.

  4. Adjust as the month happens

    Real life will change the plan. When you overspend in one category, move money from another. That's not failing the budget. That's using it.

Tools worth knowing about

You don't need an app to do any of this. A notes app or a simple list works just as well. The tool matters less than the habit. But if you want one, these support the give-every-dollar-a-job approach:

  • YNAB (You Need A Budget)Built around giving every dollar a job before you spend it. Free for college students for one year. ynab.com (opens in a new tab)
  • Copilot MoneyA separate company from Microsoft, with no connection to your Office 365 account. Automatic spending tracking. Paid subscription. copilot.money (opens in a new tab)
  • Your bank's appMost banks have built-in spending tracking and category views. Free, and already connected to your accounts.
  • A spreadsheetExcel, free with your KCTCS Office 365 account, or Google Sheets. Flexible and completely private.

AI as a money thinking partner

AI Literacy BE Safe BE Responsible

Microsoft Copilot, available with your KCTCS account, can be a useful thinking partner for money questions. It isn't a budgeting app, and it doesn't connect to your bank. Use it to explore ideas and come up with questions, not to manage your money.

  • Understanding ideas: "Explain what an emergency fund is and why it matters." "What's the difference in risk between a debit card and a credit card?"
  • Finding questions to ask: "What questions should I ask the financial aid office about when my aid arrives?"
  • Thinking through categories: "What spending categories should a college student track?" Use general examples, not your actual numbers.
  • Exploring options: "What are some free ways to track spending without an app?"

Never share personal financial information with an AI tool: account numbers, Social Security numbers, income, debt amounts, or anything you wouldn't post publicly. Use general descriptions and practice numbers only. "I spend a lot on food," not "I spend $347 a month on groceries." Then check its suggestions against your real situation before you act on them.

Financial wellness doesn't start with a perfect budget. It starts with seeing clearly: what comes in, what goes out, and where the gaps are between what you spend and what you value. Then you give your money a plan.

One honest look, and one plan that adds up to zero, is a real step forward.
