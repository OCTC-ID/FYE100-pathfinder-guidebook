Lesson 3.4

The Multitasking Myth

You're not doing two things at once. You're switching really fast, and paying for it.

About 4 minutes 6 of 10 in this module

Here's something most people believe about themselves: "I'm good at multitasking." And here's what the research says: multitasking, the way we mean it, doesn't really exist.

What we call multitasking is rapid task-switching. Your brain moves back and forth between tasks in quick succession. It isn't doing two things at once. It's doing one thing, then another, then back to the first, and every switch costs something.

You're not multitasking. You're task-switching. And every switch has a price.

The cost nobody talks about

When you switch from one task to another, your brain doesn't just pick up where it left off. It has to reorient: figure out where it was, what it was doing, and what comes next. Researchers call this the switching cost, and it adds up faster than you'd expect.

Research on interruptions has found that it can take more than 20 minutes to get back to a task after being pulled away from it. So every time you stop to check your phone, reply to a message, or flip between tabs, you're not losing seconds. You may be losing much more.

Key Term

Switching cost: the time and mental effort it takes to get back up to speed after you move from one task to another.

Does any of this sound familiar?

  • Studying"I'll just check this notification real quick…" Fifteen minutes later, you're somewhere completely different.
  • In classYou're half-listening while scrolling, then realize you missed something important and have no idea when it happened.
  • Working on an assignmentYou have three tabs open, a text conversation going, and the TV on, and nothing feels like it's getting done.
  • Getting ready to startYou've been "about to start" for 45 minutes, but every time you almost begin, something pulls you away again.

Transition friction: why switching is harder than it looks

Beyond the switching cost, there's something else worth naming: transition friction. It's the resistance your brain feels when it moves from one task to another: the mental drag of leaving something unfinished, reorienting to something new, and rebuilding momentum from scratch.

For most people, transition friction is a minor inconvenience. For some brains, including those with ADHD or other executive function differences, it can feel like a wall. The task isn't hard. Getting started on it after switching away from something else is.

Key Term

Transition friction: the resistance you feel when moving from one task, place, or mental state to another.

If this lands harder for you

If switching between tasks is noticeably more disruptive for you than it seems to be for others, that's real, and it's worth paying attention to. Some things that help:

  • Finish a thought before you switch. Before you respond to a notification or move to a new task, write down exactly where you are and what comes next. Even one sentence makes coming back much easier.
  • Build transitions into your plan. Don't schedule tasks back to back with no buffer. Give yourself five minutes between subjects, places, or types of work. That time isn't wasted. It's necessary.
  • Batch your interruptions. Instead of answering every notification as it arrives, pick specific times to check messages. Your focus stays intact in between.
Pathfinder Tip

Remember the phone face-down rule from Lesson 3.3? It isn't just about willpower. Every notification that pulls you away is a task switch, and a switching cost. Face down and out of reach means fewer switches.

You don't need to eliminate all task-switching. That isn't realistic. But noticing when you're doing it, and what it costs you, is the first step toward protecting the focus you actually have.

That's the whole lesson. Just notice it.
