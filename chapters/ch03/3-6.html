Lesson 3.6

Your Brain, Your Way

Some brains work differently. That's not a flaw, but it does matter.

About 9 minutes 8 of 10 in this module

Most time management advice assumes a particular kind of brain: one that starts tasks without much friction, stays focused with reasonable effort, tracks time naturally, and moves between activities without losing the thread.

If that sounds like you, great. If it doesn't, if you've spent years wondering why the standard strategies don't work the way everyone says they should, this lesson is for you.

It's also for everyone else. Understanding that brains work differently makes you a better student, a better teammate, and eventually a better colleague and leader.

If you've ever been told you're lazy when you were trying harder than anyone knew…

If you've ever started a task ten times without getting past the first line…

If you've ever looked up and realized three hours had passed without you noticing, or felt like you'd been staring at something for three hours when it had only been ten minutes…

If deadlines feel unreal until they're dangerously close…

If you work brilliantly in some conditions and can barely function in others…

This course sees you. And this lesson is specifically for you.

What neurodivergence actually means

Neurodivergence is an umbrella term for brains that process, learn, and function differently from what's considered neurotypical, the brain type most school and workplace systems were designed around. It includes ADHD, autism, dyslexia, dyspraxia, anxiety disorders, sensory processing differences, and more.

Some neurodivergent people have a formal diagnosis. Many don't, because they were never evaluated, because their differences were masked or compensated for, or because they're only now recognizing what's been true about them all along. You don't need a diagnosis for this lesson to apply to you.

What neurodivergence is not is a measure of intelligence, ability, or potential. Some of the most capable people you'll ever meet have brains that work very differently from the standard model. The challenge usually isn't what they can do. It's that the places they're in weren't designed with their brains in mind.

About this lesson

This lesson isn't a diagnostic tool. It's meant to give you language for experiences you may have carried without a name, and strategies that work for a wider range of brains than the usual advice does.

Executive function: the control center

Most of the challenges neurodivergent students face in college come back to one thing: executive function. It's the set of mental skills that manage how you plan, start, organize, focus, handle emotions, and shift between tasks. Think of it as the brain's control center, or, to borrow from Lesson 3.3, the cockpit.

For some brains, that system works less reliably. Not because of laziness or not caring, but because of how the brain is wired. These challenges are real, common, and worth naming.

Key Term

Executive function: the mental skills you use to plan, start, organize, focus, manage emotions, and shift between tasks.

Task initiation: starting is truly hard

For many neurodivergent brains, starting a task, especially one that feels uncertain, boring, or overwhelming, takes far more effort than it looks like from the outside. This isn't procrastination in the casual sense. It's real friction that can make the gap between "I need to do this" and "I'm doing this" feel impossible to close.

What it looks like

  • Sitting with an assignment open for an hour without producing anything
  • Starting and restarting without getting traction
  • Feeling frozen by a task you're fully capable of doing
  • Needing a deadline crisis to get going, and then doing great work under pressure

Time blindness: time feels different

Many neurodivergent people, especially those with ADHD, experience time differently. There are really two times: now and not now. Future deadlines don't feel real until they become now. This isn't a choice. It's a difference in perception that makes traditional planning advice unhelpful.

What it looks like

  • Deadlines that seemed far away suddenly arriving as emergencies
  • Losing track of time completely when you're into something
  • Regularly underestimating how long tasks take
  • Time seeming to pass at different speeds depending on the situation

Working memory: holding things in mind

Working memory is your brain's ability to hold information for a moment while you use it, like a mental sticky note. When working memory is limited or unreliable, things slip: instructions get lost, multi-step tasks fall apart, and the thought you had two seconds ago is gone before you can use it.

What it looks like

  • Walking into a room and immediately forgetting why
  • Losing your train of thought mid-sentence
  • Reading a paragraph and retaining almost nothing
  • Needing to write everything down, or it's gone

Emotional regulation: feelings hit differently

Many neurodivergent people feel emotions more intensely and have a harder time settling them quickly. Frustration, rejection, and overwhelm can hit hard and linger. This isn't immaturity. It's a difference in how the brain processes and manages emotion.

What it looks like

  • A critical comment landing harder than it "should"
  • Emotional reactions that feel bigger than the situation
  • Having a hard time bouncing back after something goes wrong
  • Avoiding tasks or situations that might bring frustration or failure

Transitions: switching gears takes effort

Moving from one task, place, or state of mind to another can take real effort for some brains. When you're deeply focused, sometimes called hyperfocus, switching away feels disruptive. And getting started on something new after a transition means rebuilding momentum from scratch.

What it looks like

  • Having trouble stopping a task even when you need to move on
  • Needing extra time and energy to shift between subjects or places
  • Feeling derailed after an interruption and struggling to get back on track
  • Hyperfocusing on one thing at the expense of everything else

Strategies that work for more brains

These strategies aren't only for neurodivergent students. They tend to work for a wider range of people than the standard advice does. Try what fits. Leave what doesn't.

For task initiation

  • Body doubling. Work near another person, even silently. Many brains get going more easily with someone else nearby: the library, a coffee shop, a study group that doesn't talk.
  • The smallest possible start. Not "write the paper," but "open the document and type one sentence." Set the bar so low that starting is almost impossible to resist.
  • Transition rituals. A specific song, a cup of something, a short walk before you sit down. It signals to your brain that work is starting.

For time blindness

  • Make time visible. Analog clocks, timers you can see, time-tracking apps. If time is invisible, your brain won't track it, so make it physical and present.
  • Time anchors. Attach tasks to events instead of clock times. "After lunch, I'll do this" is more reliable than "at 1:00."
  • Work backward from deadlines. If something is due Friday, when does it need to be done for that to happen? Set your own earlier deadline and make it real.

For working memory

  • Get it out of your head. Don't trust your brain to hold it. Write it down the moment it appears: checklists, voice memos, sticky notes, whatever you'll actually use.
  • Close the loops. Unfinished tasks create mental background noise. When something is done, mark it done. When something can't be done now, write it down so your brain can let it go.
  • One thing at a time. Single-tasking protects working memory. Every task switch costs more than you think.

For emotional regulation

  • Name it to tame it. Labeling an emotion, out loud or in writing, makes it less intense. "I'm frustrated" is easier to handle than a wave of something you can't name.
  • Build in recovery time. After something emotionally hard, you may need more time than others to reset. Plan for it instead of fighting it.
  • Know your triggers. What situations, times of day, or kinds of feedback reliably knock you off course? Knowing ahead of time lets you prepare instead of react.

For transitions

  • Warn yourself. Five minutes before a switch, acknowledge it's coming: "In five minutes, I'm switching to math." Sudden transitions are harder than prepared ones.
  • Build buffers. Don't schedule tasks back to back. Give yourself time between classes, subjects, and places.
  • Leave a breadcrumb. Before you stop working, write one sentence about where you left off and what comes next. Restarting gets much easier.

For your environment

  • Know what your brain needs. Silence or background noise? Movement or stillness? Natural light or controlled light? Experiment on purpose instead of defaulting to what you think you should need.
  • Protect your best conditions. When you find a place where you work well, treat it like a resource. Go back to it. Protect it.
  • Simplify your setup. The more decisions you make before starting, the less energy you have for the work itself.

If any of this sounds like you, there's support

OCTC's Accessibility Services office helps students whose brains work differently get the accommodations and support they need to succeed. You don't need a diagnosis to start a conversation with them, and you don't need to have it all figured out.

Accommodations might include extended time on tests, a different testing environment, note-taking support, or other adjustments that level the playing field without lowering the bar. They aren't shortcuts. They're tools that let your real abilities show.

If you've spent years compensating, masking, or wondering why things are harder for you than they seem to be for others, it's worth making that appointment.

The academic system was designed for a particular kind of brain. If yours works differently, and many do, that doesn't mean you don't belong here. It means you may need different strategies, different environments, and different support than the standard advice offers.

You've probably already figured out more about how your brain works than you realize. This lesson is an invitation to get more intentional about that knowledge: to use it, build on it, and stop apologizing for it.

Your brain is the one you have. Learn it. Work with it. It will take you further than working against it ever could.
