Appreciative Advising in FYE 100

The six phases, where each one lives in the course, and your part in it.

About 10 minutes 5 of 5 in this guide

FYE 100 was built around Appreciative Advising from the start. The six phases describe the path students take through the course, and your teaching is what moves them along it.

The course is part of OCTC's Appreciative Campus Project, which the Dean of Students oversees. This part introduces the model for instructors who are new to it, shows where each phase lives in the course, and suggests what you can do in each one.

None of this vocabulary appears on student pages. Students experience the phases without needing the names.

The appreciative mindset

Appreciative Advising starts with a habit of attention: look first for what is working. When a student is struggling, it is natural to go straight to the problem and hand over a fix. The appreciative approach begins with the student's strengths and uses them to work on the problem.

Here is the difference in three situations you will meet in FYE 100:

  • Deficit-focused"You've missed two Mile Markers. What happened?"
    Appreciative"You turned in four Mile Markers on time. What was working those weeks? Let's use that for the two that are missing."
  • Deficit-focused"This STAR(T) story is too vague."
    Appreciative"The part where you called your Success Coach is the strongest moment here. Tell me more about what you did next."
  • Deficit-focused"You need to go to tutoring."
    Appreciative"What has helped you in a class like this before? Here's who can help with that, and here's how to reach them."

Appreciative does not mean easy. The model ends with a phase called Don't Settle, and it asks for high expectations all the way through. You are still naming what is missing. You start from what the student has already shown they can do.

Where it comes from

Jennifer Bloom, Bryant Hutson, and Ye He introduced the six-phase model in The Appreciative Advising Revolution (2008). It draws on Appreciative Inquiry, positive psychology, and social constructivism.

The six phases

The phases run in order, and they also loop. A student who reaches Don't Settle sets a higher goal, and the cycle starts again.

  1. Disarm. Make a warm first impression so students feel safe.
  2. Discover. Ask open questions to learn students' strengths and stories.
  3. Dream. Invite students to share their hopes for the future.
  4. Design. Build a plan together to reach those hopes.
  5. Deliver. Students carry out the plan, with your support.
  6. Don't Settle. Challenge students, and yourself, to raise the bar.

The course as a six-phase journey

Each phase below says what it is for, what students are doing during that stretch of the course, and what you can do to help. The "Try saying" lines are examples to adapt for an announcement, a comment on a Mile Marker, or a conversation after class.

  1. 1

    Disarm

    Modules 1 and 2

    Put students at ease so they are open to learning. Many arrive expecting college to be intimidating, and first impressions form fast.

    What students are doing

    Getting oriented, naming why they are here in Mile Marker #1, mapping a support team, meeting their Success Coach, and practicing how to ask for help (Modules 1 and 2).

    Your part

    • Welcome students by name in your first announcement and your first replies.
    • Answer early messages quickly and warmly. The first reply sets the tone for the term.
    • Share a little of your own first-term story when it fits.
    • Treat asking for help as a normal thing capable students do.

    Try saying "I'm glad you're in this section. What would make this a good term for you?"

  2. 2

    Discover

    Modules 3, 4, 5, and 7

    Learn who the student is and what they already do well. Positive, open-ended questions bring out strengths, skills, and stories.

    What students are doing

    Finding out where their time and energy go (Module 3), how they study (Module 4), where their money goes (Module 5), and what their strengths and values are (Module 7). The Appreciative Advising Inventory, given in Module 5, belongs here too.

    Your part

    • Ask open questions in your feedback, and follow up on the answers.
    • Name the strengths you see in what students report, especially ones they did not name themselves.
    • When a student describes a rough patch, ask what they learned from it. That retelling is the groundwork for STAR(T) Stories.

    Try saying "Your time log shows you studied every morning before work. What made that stick?"

  3. 3

    Dream

    Module 8

    Invite students to say what they hope for, in their career and their life, and take the answer seriously.

    What students are doing

    Researching one career in depth, testing it for fit against their strengths and values, and asking what future that career would make possible (Module 8).

    Your part

    • Respond to the dream before you respond to the obstacles.
    • Connect it to what the student discovered earlier: their strengths, values, and interests.
    • When something does not line up, raise it as a question the student can explore.

    Try saying "You said helping people one-on-one is one of your top values. Where does that show up in the career you researched?"

  4. 4

    Design

    Modules 6 and 9

    Work with the student to build a plan that turns the dream into steps. The goal is to teach students how to plan and decide, so they can do it again without you.

    What students are doing

    Drafting an academic plan and writing questions for their Success Coach (Module 6), then learning the 4C Check and drafting a first STAR(T) story (Module 9).

    Your part

    • Plan with students. Ask what they would choose and why before you offer an answer.
    • Break big goals into small next steps.
    • Watch your vocabulary. "Credit hour," "prerequisite," and "AAS" are new words to many first-term students.
    • Make referrals specific: a person, a place, and what to ask for.

    Try saying "Which part of this plan are you least sure about? Let's figure out who can answer that."

  5. 5

    Deliver

    Modules 10, 11, and 12

    Students carry out the plan. Your confidence in them matters, and so does being honest that there will be obstacles and more than one way around them.

    What students are doing

    Finishing their Merit activities and naming how they work with others (Module 10), taking a first step in a community they belong to and choosing their four stories (Module 11), then telling those stories in the capstone (Module 12).

    Your part

    • Say plainly that you believe the student can do it, and say why.
    • Follow up on what students said they would do.
    • Remind students that a setback means trying another route.
    • End the course the way you started it: warmly, and by name.

    Try saying "You chose your four stories last week. Which one are you most ready to tell, and what's in the way of the others?"

  6. 6

    Don't Settle

    Module 12, and all term

    Keep raising the bar. Support and challenge go together: recognize what the student did well, then ask for more.

    What students are doing

    Looking back at their Starting Point, comparing their two Skills Snapshots, writing the Transfer step of each STAR(T) story, and planning how to finish the rest of the term (Module 12).

    Your part

    • Praise the specific action, then ask for the next level.
    • Use this phase in feedback all term. It is where "good enough" becomes "your best."
    • Hold the same standard for yourself. The model asks instructors to keep improving too.

    Try saying "This answer is solid. What would make it one you'd be proud to say out loud in an interview?"

Why Module 6 comes early

The academic plan in Module 6 is Design work, and it arrives before students have named their strengths (Module 7) or researched a career (Module 8). That order is deliberate. Students need a plan in hand when registration opens.

Treat the Module 6 plan as a first draft. After Modules 7 and 8, invite students to look at it again: does the plan still fit what they have learned about themselves? Going back to an earlier phase with better information is how the model is meant to work.

Don't Settle runs all term

Module 12 is where students look back and set their next goal, but the phase belongs in your feedback from the first Mile Marker on. Each time you recognize what a student did well and then ask for a little more, you are practicing it.

Virtual meet-ups

Merit asks students to attend three activities. Students who are on campus during the day can walk into an event. Online and commuting students mostly choose on-demand options, which they can finish from anywhere, but alone. A virtual meet-up is the one Merit option that works from anywhere and still puts students with other people. It is Disarm and Discover in practice: students meet you and each other, and you learn who they are.

Taking part is up to you. There are two ways to do it, and the first one costs you nothing.

Tier 1

Announce the shared sessions

Each term, a small number of virtual sessions are open to every FYE 100 student, whatever their section. They are run by whoever has a session ready. A Life Design Workshop is one example. The Dean of Student Success maintains the calendar.

  • Your part. Announce the sessions to your students, and remind them as each one comes up.
  • Why it works. One instructor can't offer evening, midday, and weekend times. A shared calendar can, and that is what lets a student who works evenings finish Merit.
  • Have an idea for a session? Contact the Dean of Student Success to propose it.
Tier 2

Host your own

If you'd like to host a meet-up for your own section, 30 minutes is enough. You don't need approval. Instructors have discretion over what counts for Merit, so a meet-up you host counts.

  1. Welcome and introductions, about 5 minutes. Have everyone share their name and one thing about their week.
  2. One topic, about 15 minutes. Pick something students are dealing with right now. See Topics that work.
  3. Open questions, about 10 minutes. Let students ask you, and each other, whatever is on their minds.

Use Microsoft Teams. FYE 100 courses connect to Teams through Microsoft Education, so you can create the meeting inside your course. Students join from the course, with no separate link or login.

Merit proof

  • A meet-up counts as a virtual activity, so students attach proof instead of signing a roster.
  • Tell students to take a screenshot of the meeting window during the session. Proving attendance afterward is much harder.
  • If you need to confirm who attended, Teams can give you an attendance report.

Topics that work

The Merit reflection asks students what they attended, what they learned or now think about differently, and how it connects to their goals. A session that covers only logistics gives students little to say about the last two. Choose a topic students can think about:

  • What to do when a plan stops working
  • How to decide whether to withdraw from a course
  • What this term has taught you so far
  • Taking stock of what you've already finished

In a series of sessions, include at least one on taking stock. Students are good at coming up with options, but many don't feel they are making progress. Looking at what they have already done helps.

Make it worth showing up for. A session that sounds useful or fun draws more students than one that sounds like a requirement.

The Appreciative Advising Inventory

Students complete the Appreciative Advising Inventory in Module 5. The Inventory is not in the book.

  • Your part. Make sure every student completes it, and tell them what it is: a look at their strengths and assets. It is not a test, and there are no wrong answers.
  • Where the results go. Results are compiled into a report for the Dean of Students, who uses it to guide the Appreciative Campus Project.
  • How results shape the course. Inventory results feed back into course design. When an item shows a gap, a lesson or Mile Marker may be adjusted to work on it.

Results in action

Each callout below shows one Inventory result and the course change it led to. New ones are added as results come in.

Results in action

Mile Marker #2, Question 1: three people at OCTC

What the Inventory showed
One item asks whether students know at least three people who work at the college they can go to for advice and support. Students arrive with family and friends behind them, but this item lags. After the Success Coach lookup was added to Module 2, it rose more than any other item.
What changed
Question 1 now asks for three people at OCTC, at least two who work here, so the assignment works on the same thing the item measures. Family and friends moved to an optional part.
When you grade
  • Three people at OCTC, at least two who work here. Instructors, advisors, tutors, peer mentors, and office staff all count as working here. A classmate can be the third.
  • The Success Coach by name is one of the three.
  • You count. If a student names you, their FYE instructor, because they feel connected, that's a real answer.
  • A person, not an office. "The Library" isn't a person. "The librarian who helped me find sources" is, even without a name. Early in the term, who they are and where to find them is enough.
  • Family and friends belong in the optional "Who else is in your corner?" part. They're welcome, but they don't count toward the three.

Results in action

Lookout: Ask Your Success Coach

What the Inventory showed
About one in five students could not name their Success Coach when they took the Inventory, even though Lesson 2.2 had asked them to look the coach up. These students scored well below their peers on knowing people at the college, feeling valued by other students, and taking part in campus life. Students who lose track of their coach tend to be the least connected students overall. The item on knowing three people at the college rose more than any other item after the Module 2 lookup was added. A specific assignment moved that item. General statements that help is available had not.
What changed
A new recurring element, the Lookout, marks content about the Success Coach. It uses an owl icon, a navy header band, and the heading "Lookout: Ask Your Success Coach." Every heading uses the term "Success Coach" because that is the term students are not retaining. Each placement covers a different way a coach can help, and each ends with a link to Lesson 2.2.
  • Lesson 3.2: when a time plan isn't working. Covers withdrawing, a possible bi-term course, and next semester's course load.
  • Lesson 4.2: when new study strategies aren't improving a grade. Covers tutoring, Accessibility Services, and whether the grade can still be raised.
  • Register Early Trail Tip: schedule changes after classes start.
  • Finishing Strong Trail Tip: students can keep working with the coach after FYE 100 ends, including on decisions that come up late in the term.
When you teach
  • Use the title. When a student brings you a problem with a schedule, a grade, or a withdrawal, refer them to their Success Coach by name or title, not just to "advising."
  • Send aid questions to the coach. Withdrawals and schedule changes can affect financial aid, so refer students to the coach before they act instead of answering aid questions yourself.
  • Remember the end of the term. FYE 100 ends before full-term courses do. After that, the coach is the student's main continuing support through finals, incompletes, and registration.
  • Keep it to five placements. If the element appeared everywhere, students would stop reading it. Each Lookout is placed where a struggling student needs it, not to move an Inventory item.

Results in action

Virtual meet-ups

What the Inventory showed
Campus participation is the lowest item in the Inventory, and it is the only item where a large share of students actively disagree. The items that move with it are about contact with people, not about finishing activities: knowing three people at the college, and feeling valued by other students. Early College students, who are on campus during the day, score far higher on all three. Connection seems to come from being around other people. An earlier awareness campaign did not move campus participation, so awareness was not the problem.
What changed
This guide now has a section on virtual meet-ups: a shared calendar of sessions any FYE 100 student can attend, and a simple plan for hosting your own. On-demand options are the easiest to fit in, but students complete them alone. A meet-up is the one option that is both flexible and connected. If every Merit activity can be done alone, participation numbers may rise while connection does not.
When you teach
  • Announce the shared sessions. That is the only ask.
  • Hosting is up to you. Nothing here is required of you or your students.
  • Point students to live options. When a student asks which Merit activity to choose, a meet-up is the one that also helps them get to know people.
