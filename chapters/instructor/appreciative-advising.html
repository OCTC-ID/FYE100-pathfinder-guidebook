Appreciative Advising in FYE 100

The six phases, where each one lives in the course, and your part in it.

About 10 minutes 5 of 5 in this guide

FYE 100 was built around Appreciative Advising from the start. The six phases describe the path students take through the course, and your teaching is what moves them along it.

The course is part of OCTC's Appreciative Campus Project, which the Dean of Students oversees. This part introduces the model for instructors who are new to it, shows where each phase lives in the course, and suggests what you can do in each one.

None of this vocabulary appears on student pages. Students experience the phases without needing the names.

Or save it as a PDF from the print window.

The appreciative mindset

Appreciative Advising starts with a habit of attention: look first for what is working. When a student is struggling, it is natural to go straight to the problem and hand over a fix. The appreciative approach begins with the student's strengths and uses them to work on the problem.

Here is the difference in three situations you will meet in FYE 100:

  • Deficit-focused"You've missed two Mile Markers. What happened?"
    Appreciative"You turned in four Mile Markers on time. What was working those weeks? Let's use that for the two that are missing."
  • Deficit-focused"This STAR(T) story is too vague."
    Appreciative"The part where you called your Success Coach is the strongest moment here. Tell me more about what you did next."
  • Deficit-focused"You need to go to tutoring."
    Appreciative"What has helped you in a class like this before? Here's who can help with that, and here's how to reach them."

Appreciative does not mean easy. The model ends with a phase called Don't Settle, and it asks for high expectations all the way through. You are still naming what is missing. You start from what the student has already shown they can do.

Where it comes from

Jennifer Bloom, Bryant Hutson, and Ye He introduced the six-phase model in The Appreciative Advising Revolution (2008). It draws on Appreciative Inquiry, positive psychology, and social constructivism.

The six phases

The phases run in order, and they also loop. A student who reaches Don't Settle sets a higher goal, and the cycle starts again.

  1. Disarm. Make a warm first impression so students feel safe.
  2. Discover. Ask open questions to learn students' strengths and stories.
  3. Dream. Invite students to share their hopes for the future.
  4. Design. Build a plan together to reach those hopes.
  5. Deliver. Students carry out the plan, with your support.
  6. Don't Settle. Challenge students, and yourself, to raise the bar.

The course as a six-phase journey

Each phase below says what it is for, what students are doing during that stretch of the course, and what you can do to help. The "Try saying" lines are examples to adapt for an announcement, a comment on a Mile Marker, or a conversation after class.

  1. 1

    Disarm

    Modules 1 and 2

    Put students at ease so they are open to learning. Many arrive expecting college to be intimidating, and first impressions form fast.

    What students are doing

    Getting oriented, naming why they are here in Mile Marker #1, mapping a support team, meeting their Success Coach, and practicing how to ask for help (Modules 1 and 2).

    Your part

    • Welcome students by name in your first announcement and your first replies.
    • Answer early messages quickly and warmly. The first reply sets the tone for the term.
    • Share a little of your own first-term story when it fits.
    • Treat asking for help as a normal thing capable students do.

    Try saying "I'm glad you're in this section. What would make this a good term for you?"

  2. 2

    Discover

    Modules 3, 4, 5, and 7

    Learn who the student is and what they already do well. Positive, open-ended questions bring out strengths, skills, and stories.

    What students are doing

    Finding out where their time and energy go (Module 3), how they study (Module 4), where their money goes (Module 5), and what their strengths and values are (Module 7). The Appreciative Advising Inventory belongs here too.

    Your part

    • Ask open questions in your feedback, and follow up on the answers.
    • Name the strengths you see in what students report, especially ones they did not name themselves.
    • When a student describes a rough patch, ask what they learned from it. That retelling is the groundwork for STAR(T) Stories.

    Try saying "Your time log shows you studied every morning before work. What made that stick?"

  3. 3

    Dream

    Module 8

    Invite students to say what they hope for, in their career and their life, and take the answer seriously.

    What students are doing

    Researching one career in depth, testing it for fit against their strengths and values, and asking what future that career would make possible (Module 8).

    Your part

    • Respond to the dream before you respond to the obstacles.
    • Connect it to what the student discovered earlier: their strengths, values, and interests.
    • When something does not line up, raise it as a question the student can explore.

    Try saying "You said helping people one-on-one is one of your top values. Where does that show up in the career you researched?"

  4. 4

    Design

    Modules 6 and 9

    Work with the student to build a plan that turns the dream into steps. The goal is to teach students how to plan and decide, so they can do it again without you.

    What students are doing

    Drafting an academic plan and writing questions for their Success Coach (Module 6), then learning the 4C Check and drafting a first STAR(T) story (Module 9).

    Your part

    • Plan with students. Ask what they would choose and why before you offer an answer.
    • Break big goals into small next steps.
    • Watch your vocabulary. "Credit hour," "prerequisite," and "AAS" are new words to many first-term students.
    • Make referrals specific: a person, a place, and what to ask for.

    Try saying "Which part of this plan are you least sure about? Let's figure out who can answer that."

  5. 5

    Deliver

    Modules 10, 11, and 12

    Students carry out the plan. Your confidence in them matters, and so does being honest that there will be obstacles and more than one way around them.

    What students are doing

    Finishing their Merit activities and naming how they work with others (Module 10), taking a first step in a community they belong to and choosing their four stories (Module 11), then telling those stories in the capstone (Module 12).

    Your part

    • Say plainly that you believe the student can do it, and say why.
    • Follow up on what students said they would do.
    • Remind students that a setback means trying another route.
    • End the course the way you started it: warmly, and by name.

    Try saying "You chose your four stories last week. Which one are you most ready to tell, and what's in the way of the others?"

  6. 6

    Don't Settle

    Module 12, and all term

    Keep raising the bar. Support and challenge go together: recognize what the student did well, then ask for more.

    What students are doing

    Looking back at their Starting Point, comparing their two Skills Snapshots, writing the Transfer step of each STAR(T) story, and planning how to finish the rest of the term (Module 12).

    Your part

    • Praise the specific action, then ask for the next level.
    • Use this phase in feedback all term. It is where "good enough" becomes "your best."
    • Hold the same standard for yourself. The model asks instructors to keep improving too.

    Try saying "This answer is solid. What would make it one you'd be proud to say out loud in an interview?"

Why Module 6 comes early

The academic plan in Module 6 is Design work, and it arrives before students have named their strengths (Module 7) or researched a career (Module 8). That order is deliberate. Students need a plan in hand when registration opens.

Treat the Module 6 plan as a first draft. After Modules 7 and 8, invite students to look at it again: does the plan still fit what they have learned about themselves? Going back to an earlier phase with better information is how the model is meant to work.

Don't Settle runs all term

Module 12 is where students look back and set their next goal, but the phase belongs in your feedback from the first Mile Marker on. Each time you recognize what a student did well and then ask for a little more, you are practicing it.

The Appreciative Advising Inventory

Students complete the Appreciative Advising Inventory in Module 5. The Inventory is not in the book.

  • Your part. Make sure every student completes it, and tell them what it is: a look at their strengths and assets. It is not a test, and there are no wrong answers.
  • Where the results go. Results are compiled into a report for the Dean of Students, who uses it to guide the Appreciative Campus Project.
