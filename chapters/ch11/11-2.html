Lesson 11.2

What Is Community, Really?

You're already in more communities than you think, and already shaping how they work.

About 9 minutes 3 of 7 in this module

You're already in more communities than you might think, and you're already shaping how they work.

In the last lesson, you looked at Civic Engagement and the official language behind it. This lesson is where that language stops sounding official and starts sounding like your actual life.

You're already in community

When people hear "community," they often picture something big and a little distant: a city, a cause, an organization you'd have to go join. That's one kind of community, and honestly, not the most useful one to start with.

Think about the spaces you're already part of:

  • The people you live with
  • The people you work with
  • Your classes
  • A team, club, or group chat
  • Your neighborhood
  • Online spaces and interest groups

Each of these is a community. You're in several at once, and you're already shaping how they work, through what you do, what you don't do, and what you let slide.

Community isn't something you have to go find. It's something you're already standing in.

Every community runs on a system

Here's the part that doesn't get said often enough: every one of those spaces runs on a system. Nobody necessarily designed it on purpose, but it's there anyway. A system, in this sense, has four parts:

  • People

    Who's actually involved, and what role each person tends to play.

  • Rules

    The official ones, and the unwritten ones everyone just... knows.

  • Influence

    Who actually shapes what happens. It isn't always the person with the title.

  • Leverage points

    The small things that, if they changed, would change a lot else.

Once you start looking for these four things, you see them everywhere. That's a useful skill, not just for civic life in some abstract sense, but for navigating any group you're part of, starting today.

Worked example: the shared kitchen

Why this counts as civic engagement

Remember the Milestone description from the last lesson: engages with others who represent different perspectives, through active listening, clear and responsive dialogue, and collaboration. That's official language, written for a statewide rubric. But it describes exactly what happened in the kitchen. Maya didn't do anything political. She paid attention to how the situation looked to everyone, said something clear and direct, and opened the door to figuring it out together.

Seeing the system, noticing who has influence (including yourself), finding the leverage point, and acting on it without making it bigger than it needs to be: those are the skills civic engagement is pointing at. They scale. The same skills that help in a shared kitchen help in a workplace, a student organization, a neighborhood association, and, eventually, civic life in the broadest sense.

Look back at

The 4C Check. Seeing a system clearly is the same move as Clarity and Context: step back and see what's actually going on before you react.

Your turn

Pick one community you're part of right now: a living situation, a job, a class, a team, a group chat. Then work through four questions.

  1. People

    Who's actually involved, and what role does each person tend to play?

  2. Rules

    What's one unwritten rule running the show, something everyone seems to know even though nobody agreed to it out loud?

  3. Influence

    Who has the most real influence over how this community works? It might not be who you'd expect, and it might be you.

  4. Leverage point

    If you wanted one small thing to work better, what's one small action that might actually shift it?

You don't need to act on any of this yet. Just notice it, and write it down in Steps 1 and 2 of the Community Snapshot. You'll use it for Mile Marker #11.

AI Literacy BE Responsible

If you want a thinking partner

Talking the four questions through with an AI tool can help you notice patterns you hadn't put into words. Try: "I'm trying to think through a group I'm part of: [describe it briefly, no names]. Ask me questions that help me notice the unwritten rules and who has influence."

It's a thinking tool, not a way to skip the noticing. AI has no way to know what's really going on in your kitchen, your job, or your group chat. The community you pick, and what you notice about it, has to come from you.

If this kind of thinking interests you

People, rules, influence, and leverage points are a small taste of systems thinking: a way of looking at almost anything, from a household to a workplace to a city or an ecosystem, and asking how its parts connect and influence each other.

You don't need to study it further for this course. But if the kitchen example clicked, systems thinking is a real field with a lot more to explore, and it shows up in areas from environmental science to organizational design to public policy.

Next, you'll turn this lens on yourself: what you have to offer the communities you're part of, and on what terms.
