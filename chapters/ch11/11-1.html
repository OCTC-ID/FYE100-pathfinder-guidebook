Skill Spotlight: Civic Engagement

Starting with the parts of "society" you're already standing in.

About 7 minutes 2 of 7 in this module
7
10 Essential Skills · #7

Civic Engagement

What this skill is

Engaging in civic life to improve society, starting with the parts of "society" you're already standing in.

"Civic engagement" tends to bring certain images to mind: voting, city council meetings, protests, campaigns. Those are real examples. They can also feel intimidating if you're not there yet, and they can make this skill seem like something for other people, people older, more political, or more "into that kind of thing" than you.

Here's the thing: every one of those bigger examples is built on a smaller foundation. It's the everyday ability to engage with the people and systems around you, notice how things actually work, and help make a shared space better. You don't have to wait to build that. You're building it right now, in the communities you're already part of.

Benchmark civic engagement notices the conversation. Milestone civic engagement joins it, and helps move it somewhere.

Where you're headed

Here's the difference between where most students start and where you're headed. This skill's official language talks about public policy and government, because that's where it's ultimately pointing. You'll build toward it in smaller spaces first.

See also

Lesson 1.2 explains Benchmark, Milestone, and Capstone.

  1. Benchmark Where you're starting

    • You respectfully discuss basic ideas about public policy and the social good, sharing your own opinions and acknowledging others'.
    • You can explain basic democratic principles, the U.S. Constitution, and how individual and community involvement has shaped American government, past and present.
    • You can describe opportunities for people or groups to engage in civic life and bring about positive change.

  2. Milestone Your target

    • You engage with people who see public policy and the social good differently, through active listening, clear and responsive dialogue, and collaboration.
    • You use what you know about how people's participation shapes public policy to plan for action.
    • You make persuasive arguments that benefit society, based on your own experience, what you've observed, and outside knowledge or perspectives.

The shift from Benchmark to Milestone is the shift from describing how civic life works to taking part in it: engaging with people who see things differently, and using what you notice to help move something forward.

The three Milestone behaviors, up close

1Engage across different perspectives

At the Benchmark level, this looks like respectful discussion: you share your view and acknowledge that others have theirs. At the Milestone level, it becomes active. You really listen to someone who sees a shared situation differently, respond to what they actually said (not what you assumed they'd say), and work with them rather than just alongside them.

This is less about debate and more about something quieter. Can you be in a room, a group chat, or a shared living space with someone whose perspective is different from yours, and still move toward something together?

Two roommates disagree about how guests should be handled. Benchmark: both state their position and move on. Milestone: each one listens to why the other cares, and they land on something neither started with, because they built it together.

2Understand how participation shapes what happens

At the Benchmark level, this is knowledge: you can describe how people's involvement affects what happens in a system, whether that's a government or any other shared system. At the Milestone level, you use that knowledge. You look at a situation and ask, "Given how this works, what would actually make a difference here?"

This is where "civic" stops being a category of activity and becomes a way of looking at any shared space. Outcomes aren't fixed, and participation, yours and other people's, is part of what shapes them.

A student notices that group projects in their program keep falling apart the same way, semester after semester. Benchmark: they can describe why. Milestone: they use that understanding to plan something different, for their own group or by raising it with an instructor in a way that could change the pattern.

3Make a case based on real reflection

At the Benchmark level, you know opportunities to make a difference exist. At the Milestone level, you can make an actual case for something, grounded in your own experience, what you've observed, and what you've learned from other perspectives. That's more than stating an opinion. It's building an argument someone else might find persuasive.

The key word is reflection. An argument grounded in real experience and honest analysis lands differently than a generic opinion, because it's specific, and it shows your thinking, not just your conclusion.

Instead of "The campus shuttle schedule doesn't work," try: "Here's specifically when and why it doesn't work for students like me, here's who else is affected, and here's what would solve it."

Where it shows up

You don't need an official "civic" occasion to build these behaviors. They show up in the ordinary spaces you're already part of.

A group disagrees about direction

Benchmark: You share your opinion and note that others have theirs.

Milestone: You listen to why others see it differently, and work toward something that reflects more than your own view.

A problem keeps coming back

Benchmark: You can describe the pattern and why it keeps happening.

Milestone: You use that understanding to plan a different approach, for yourself or the group.

Something isn't working

Benchmark: You know there are ways to raise concerns or suggest change.

Milestone: You build a specific case, grounded in what you've noticed, and bring it somewhere it can be heard.

You notice what others miss

Benchmark: You mention it in passing, if at all.

Milestone: You connect what you noticed to other knowledge and perspectives, and use it to make your point land.

Pathfinder Tip

"Civic" doesn't have to mean "official." Every time you engage thoughtfully with people who see things differently, understand how a shared system works, and make a grounded case for something better, you're practicing civic engagement, whether or not anyone calls it that.

In your own life

Reflect
  • When was the last time you really listened to someone who saw a shared situation differently than you did?
  • What's one problem in a space you share (home, work, class, online) that keeps coming back?
  • If you had to make a case for one small change there, what would you say?

How it shows up later

This module spends some time on what "community" actually means, and you may find it's broader, and closer to home, than you expected. The skills behind Civic Engagement are available to you now, in the rooms you're already in. Civic Engagement is also an option for the fourth skill in your STAR(T) Stories.

Skill definition and Benchmark and Milestone indicators are based on the Kentucky Council on Postsecondary Education's Kentucky Graduate Profile (opens in a new tab).
