Lesson 11.3

Your Strengths, Your Community

Putting what you know about yourself to work.

About 8 minutes 4 of 7 in this module

You already know more about yourself than you've had a chance to use. This lesson is about putting it to work.

Everything you've already figured out

This term, you've built up a surprising amount of self-knowledge, usually one piece at a time, in different modules, for different reasons. Put together, it looks like this:

  • Career Coach (Module 6): the kinds of work and environments that interest you
  • HIGH5 (Module 7): your top strengths, what you're naturally good at and energized by
  • The Values Card Sort (Module 7): what actually matters to you, when you get specific
  • Your Collaboration Snapshot (Module 10): how you work with others, and what you need from a team
  • The last lesson: a lens for seeing how communities actually work

Most of the time these stay separate: a result here, a reflection there. This lesson puts them in the same room, because together they help you decide how you want to show up in your communities, on terms that fit who you are.

Why "I don't have time" is usually half the story

If you've ever been invited to get involved in something (a club, a volunteer opportunity, a cause you cared about) and said "I just don't have time," you're not alone, and you're probably not lying. But time is often the easy answer, because the real one is more complicated: I don't know what I'd be walking into, I don't want to let people down, and I'm worried I'd end up doing something I'd dread.

That's not a flaw. It's exactly what it feels like to consider stepping into a system you can't see yet, which is what the last lesson was about. And if not knowing the system is part of what makes it hard, having your own terms clear going in makes a real difference.

What I have to offer, and what I don't

Before you say yes, or no, to getting more involved in something, know two things about yourself:

What I have to offer

Based on your actual strengths, values, and interests. Not what you think you're "supposed" to offer.

What I'm not available for

At least for now, at least at first. A limit, a role you don't want, a kind of task that drains you.

Naming both before you're in the middle of something changes the whole experience. Instead of slowly discovering your limits, and maybe feeling guilty about them, you walk in with your terms already clear, to yourself and often to the people you're working with.

Worked example: Jordan and the community garden

Your turn

Go back to the community from the last lesson, or pick a different one, including something you'd like to get more involved in.

  1. What I have to offer

    Based on your strengths, values, and interests, what's something real you could bring to this community? Not what you think you "should" offer, but something that fits you.

  2. What I'm not available for

    What's one thing you'd want to be upfront about, rather than discovering it later?

  3. Saying it out loud

    How would it feel to say both of these before getting more involved?

Write your answers in Step 3 of the Community Snapshot.

If you want a thinking partner

Try: "Here's what I know about my strengths and values: [list them]. I'm thinking about getting more involved in [describe the group, no names]. Help me think through what I could genuinely offer, and what I might want to be upfront about not being available for."

Then check what comes back against what you know about yourself. Does it sound like you? The mix of strengths, values, and limits that's true for you has to come from you.
