Most students study the way they were taught to study, which often isn't how learning actually works.

Rereading notes feels productive. Highlighting feels like engagement. Cramming the night before feels like urgency. None of these is especially effective, and the research on this is consistent.

This lesson covers four strategies with strong evidence behind them. They aren't magic, and they don't work the same way for every brain. But understanding why they work helps you decide whether and how to use them in your own life.

The goal isn't to study more. It's to study in ways that stick.

Four strategies worth trying

1Retrieval practice: test yourself before the test tests you

The best-supported study strategy in learning research is also one of the least intuitive: testing yourself on material, before you feel ready, works better than reviewing it. Pulling information out of memory strengthens it in ways that rereading doesn't.

Every time you struggle to recall something and then find the answer, you build a stronger memory than if you'd just read it again. The struggle is the point.

  • Close your notes. Write down everything you remember from class without looking. Then check what you missed. The gaps are what you need to study.
  • Use flashcards, but try to recall the answer before you flip the card. Looking at both sides at once is review, not retrieval.
  • After a reading, close the book and answer: what were the three most important things I just read?
Try it and noticeAfter your next class, spend five minutes writing down what you remember without looking at your notes. Notice what came easily and what didn't. That's your study list.
[[term]] Key Term

Retrieval practice: studying by pulling information out of your memory, like quizzing yourself, instead of reading it again.

2Spaced practice: come back before you forget

Memory fades over time, and reviewing material just as it starts to fade works better than reviewing it right after you learn it. This is called the spacing effect, and it's why cramming gets you through tomorrow's test but not next month's.

You don't need a complicated system. Study something, wait a day or two, and review it again. Wait a little longer, and review again. Each time you come back, the memory gets stronger and lasts longer.

  • Spend 10 minutes on your notes the day of class. Come back two days later for another 10. Review again before the exam. Three short sessions beat one long one.
  • Keep a running list of concepts you're shaky on. Revisit it every few days instead of waiting for exam week.
Try it and noticeSchedule one 10-minute review of this week's material for two days from now, and set a reminder. Notice whether you remember more or less than you expected, and what that tells you about how spacing works for your brain.

3Elaborative interrogation: ask why until it makes sense

Elaborative interrogation means asking "why" and "how" as you study, and connecting new information to things you already know. Instead of memorizing facts, you build meaning, and meaning sticks better than isolated facts.

This strategy tends to work especially well for students whose brains are drawn to patterns and connections, and less well for those who find open-ended questions hard to pin down. If that's you, pair it with retrieval practice.

  • Reading about a historical event: don't just memorize the date. Ask why it happened when it did, and what would have had to be different for it not to happen.
  • Studying a concept in biology: don't just memorize the definition. Ask why it works this way, and what would happen if it didn't.
  • After reading anything, ask "So what?" Why does this matter? How does it connect to what I already know?
Try it and noticePick one concept from a current class. Write three "why" or "how" questions about it and try to answer them. Notice whether the concept feels clearer or more confusing afterward. Both are useful information.

4Interleaving: mix it up on purpose

Most students study one topic until it feels comfortable, then move to the next. That feels efficient, but research suggests that mixing different topics or problem types in one session leads to better long-term learning, even though it feels harder in the moment.

The difficulty is the point. When you have to figure out which approach fits which problem, your brain does more work, and that work builds understanding that lasts.

  • Instead of doing 20 math problems of the same type in a row, do 5 of one type, then 5 of another, then come back to the first. It will feel harder. That means it's working.
  • When reviewing for a test that covers several chapters, shuffle the topics instead of going in order.
Try it and noticeIn your next study session for a class with several topics, switch between them every 15 to 20 minutes. Notice how it feels compared to sticking with one. Feeling unsure is normal. That's the learning happening.

What feels productive but isn't

These are the study habits students rely on most, and why the research isn't kind to them.

  • Feels productiveRereading notes or the textbook. It builds familiarity, not learning. You recognize the material, but recognizing isn't recalling.
    [[next]]
    InsteadRetrieval practice. Close the notes. Try to recall. Then check. The effort of recalling is what builds the memory.
  • Feels productiveHighlighting everything. It's passive and feels like engagement. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
    [[next]]
    InsteadWrite questions in the margin. Next to the important parts, write a question. Later, try to answer it without looking.
  • Feels productiveCramming the night before. It fades fast. You may pass the test and remember almost nothing two weeks later.
    [[next]]
    InsteadSpaced practice. Three 20-minute sessions over three days usually beat one 60-minute session the night before.

None of these strategies works perfectly for every brain or every subject. That's information about how you learn, not proof the strategy failed. Try one. Notice what happens. Adjust.

The students who figure out how they actually learn, not how they think they should learn, are the ones who stop dreading exam week. It's not magic. It's paying attention to what works.

Keep it going: write a SMART goal

Trying a strategy once is a start. Keeping it going is where it pays off. A SMART goal turns "I should study better" into something you can do, and check.

  • S
    Specific. What exactly will you do? Name the strategy and the class.
  • M
    Measurable. How will you know you did it? A number, a count, a checkmark.
  • A
    Achievable. Is it realistic for your life right now, with your work, family, and energy?
  • R
    Relevant. Why this habit? Which class or goal does it help?
  • T
    Time-bound. When, and how often? Give it a schedule and an end point.

Not SMART"I'm going to study more for biology."

SMART"For the rest of the term, I'll spend 10 minutes quizzing myself on my biology notes every Tuesday and Thursday after class, and check it off in my planner. I'll know it's working if I can answer most of my own questions before each exam."

[[term]] Key Term

SMART goal: a goal that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound.

Using AI as a study partner

AI Literacy BE Critical

AI tools can support the strategies in this lesson when you use them to think alongside you, not instead of you. Here's what that looks like:

  • Retrieval practice: "Quiz me on [topic]. Ask me five questions and don't give me the answers until I try." Then ask yourself whether the questions match what you actually need to know.
  • Elaborative interrogation: "Explain why [concept] works the way it does, in plain language." Ask follow-up questions until it makes sense, and check the explanation against your course materials.
  • Spaced practice: "List the key points from [topic] so I can review them." Cover the list and try to recall the points before you read it.
  • Finding gaps: "What are the most commonly misunderstood parts of [topic]?" Use the answer to test your own understanding, not to replace it.

The line worth holding: using AI to quiz yourself is studying. Using AI to write your notes, your summaries, or your assignment answers is not. One builds your ability. The other skips it. You'll know the difference when you sit down for the exam.
