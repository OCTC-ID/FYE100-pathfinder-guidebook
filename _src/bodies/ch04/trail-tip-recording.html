A few years ago, recording a class took equipment. Now it takes one tap on a phone.

AI note-taking apps can join an online class, transcribe everything, and summarize the discussion before you've closed your laptop. The technology is impressive, and the questions it raises are real.

KCTCS doesn't have one system-wide recording policy. That means the rules vary by instructor, by course, and by situation, and your judgment, your professionalism, and your respect for the people in the room matter more than a rulebook. This lesson is about building that judgment.

Three principles to guide your decisions

1Respect the course: check the syllabus, and ask

Your syllabus is the contract for each course. Some instructors allow recording for personal study. Some don't allow it at all. Many don't mention it, and silence is not the same as permission.

The professional move, every time, is to ask before you record. Not after. Not while you're already recording. Before.

  • Check your syllabus first. The answer may already be there.
  • If it isn't, ask your instructor directly, before class or by email.
  • If an instructor says no, that's the answer. It isn't a negotiation.
  • Permission in one class doesn't carry over to another. Ask each instructor.

That's it: specific, professional, and honest about your reason. Most instructors appreciate being asked rather than finding out later that they were recorded.

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

If recording is part of a documented accommodation, work through Accessibility Services to get it in writing. An official accommodation is different from informal permission, and it protects you.

2Respect the people: your classmates and your instructor

A classroom is a shared space. When you record, you capture more than the instructor's voice. You capture your classmates' questions, comments, and discussion, and they didn't agree to be recorded.

This matters more than most students realize. A classmate who shares something personal, asks a question they feel unsure about, or says something they'd like to take back has a reasonable expectation that it stays in the room.

  • An instructor's lectures may be their intellectual property. Sharing recordings without permission can be a serious violation.
  • Recording classmates without their knowledge or consent raises real privacy concerns, even when it's technically easy.
  • Posting or sharing recordings anywhere outside the class, like social media or group chats, is almost always inappropriate and may violate College policy.
  • Even with permission, treat a recording as personal study material, not content to share.
[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

The test is simple: would the people being recorded be comfortable knowing it, and knowing how the recording will be used? If the answer is anything but a clear yes, don't record, or ask first.

3Respect your own learning: recording isn't learning

There's a real risk that recording replaces paying attention instead of supporting it. When you know a recording exists, your brain tends to relax, because "it's captured." But captured audio isn't understanding. Making sense of the material still takes work, and that work happens in real time, not during playback.

  • Students who rely heavily on recordings often end up with hours of audio they never revisit.
  • Taking notes as you go, even imperfect ones, helps you remember more than recording alone.
  • If you record, use it alongside your notes, not instead of them. Go back to specific moments you marked as unclear. Don't relisten to the whole thing.
  • Working to keep up in real time is part of learning. Don't hand that work off entirely.
[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

If you're recording because you can't keep up, pay attention to that. Talk to your instructor, and ask the Teaching & Learning Center about note-taking strategies. The recording may be covering a gap worth addressing directly.

AI note-takers: a separate conversation

AI Literacy BE Safe BE Honest BE Responsible

Tools like Otter.ai, Fireflies, and similar apps go beyond basic recording. They upload audio to another company's servers, process it with outside AI systems, and store transcripts in ways you may not fully control.

Before you use any AI note-taker in a class or meeting, think through these questions:

  • Who has the data? Uploading a recording sends your classmates' voices and your instructor's lecture to a company's servers. Read the privacy policy before you use it.
  • Did everyone agree? Your classmates didn't sign up to have their voices processed by an AI company. That's a real privacy concern, not a technicality.
  • Does your instructor know what the tool does? Many instructors don't realize that "I'm using an AI note-taker" means their lecture goes to an outside platform. That's a different conversation from asking to record on your phone.
  • Is the transcript accurate? AI transcription makes mistakes, some small, some serious. One misheard term or date can send you studying the wrong thing. Check it against your course materials.
  • Are you still paying attention? The same risk as recording applies here, maybe more. If the app is "handling it," your brain may check out completely.

The responsible move: treat an AI note-taker like any recording device. Ask permission, be upfront about what the tool does, and use what it produces with a critical eye. If an instructor allows recording but hasn't thought about AI note-takers, have that conversation specifically.

The habit to build here isn't a rule. It's a professional reflex: when in doubt, ask. It will serve you in every classroom, workplace, and meeting you'll ever be part of.

People who ask before acting build trust. People who act first and ask later, or never ask, wear it down. That's true whether the subject is recording a class, sharing information, or a hundred other situations you haven't met yet.

Build the habit now, while the stakes are low. It'll be there when they aren't.
