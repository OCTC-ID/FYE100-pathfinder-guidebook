We make decisions every day, some big, some small. But most of us rarely stop to ask why we choose what we choose.

Behind every real decision sits something deeper than preference or convenience: your personal values. Values are the principles and beliefs that guide how you live, often without your noticing. They shape what you prioritize, what you say yes or no to, how you react under pressure, and the people and places you're drawn to.

When your actions line up with your values, life tends to feel more meaningful and less stressful. When they don't, something feels off, even if you can't name exactly why.

Interests tell you what you enjoy. Values tell you who you are.

What values actually shape

Your values are working in the background of almost every important choice you make. Here's where they show up most clearly.

  • Your priorities

    What you actually spend time and energy on. Not what you say matters, but what you act like matters.

  • Your boundaries

    What you'll say no to, even when it's inconvenient or disappoints someone.

  • Your goals

    What you're working toward, and whether those goals feel like yours or borrowed from someone else.

  • Your reactions

    Why some situations energize you and others drain you. Often it comes down to whether your values are being honored.

  • Your relationships

    Who you're drawn to and who you clash with. Shared or clashing values explain a lot of both.

  • Your career fit

    Whether a job or field feels sustainable over time, which often has as much to do with values as with interest.

Interests and values aren't the same thing

Many career tools, like the interest assessment you took in Module 6, focus on interests: what you enjoy, what you're curious about, what keeps you engaged. Interests matter. But they're only part of the picture.

Lesson 6.2: The six interest types

Interests

  • What you enjoy or find engaging right now
  • Can shift as you try new things and meet new people
  • Often shaped by what you've been exposed to so far
  • May not carry you when the work gets hard
  • Useful for exploring directions

Values

  • What matters to you at a deeper level
  • Tend to stay steadier across time and situations
  • Often show up under pressure, when choices get hard
  • Give meaning even when the work isn't exciting
  • A more reliable compass for long-term decisions

What the research says

Research on values at work finds that when people's work fits their personal values, they tend to be more satisfied and more committed to it. Researchers also find that interests and values are related but measure different things. You can be very interested in something that clashes with your values, or committed to work that isn't exciting but feels deeply meaningful.

Sources: Schwartz (1992); Sagiv & Schwartz (1995); Dobson et al. (2014); Arieli, Sagiv, & Roccas (2020).

If your interests keep shifting

Some people move easily between interests, drawn to many subjects, fields, and activities over time. If that's you, following your interests from one thing to the next can feel exciting but directionless. Each interest feels real in the moment, but "What do I actually want to do?" stays unanswered.

For people like this, values are often the more useful compass. They connect the dots between pursuits that look unrelated. Someone who values creativity, connection, and impact might find those values showing up in education, health care, design, or community work: very different fields, with the same underlying why.

If your interests feel too varied to point anywhere, ask yourself: what do the things I keep coming back to have in common? The answer is usually a value, not an interest.

Values can shift too, and that's normal

Values tend to be steadier than interests, but they aren't fixed forever. What mattered most to you at 16 may look different at 20, 30, or 40. Big life experiences can clarify or shift what you prioritize. That isn't inconsistency. It's growth.

Looking at your values now gives you a clearer picture of who is actually showing up to college today. Not who you were, and not who you think you should be.

The Values Card Sort

The Values Card Sort helps you find your Top 5 core values, not by answering questions about yourself, but by choosing between values that all feel important. The sorting and narrowing is where the real reflection happens.

  1. Sort the cards

    Place each of 30 value cards into one of three piles: most important to you, somewhat important, and least important. Go with your gut.

  2. Narrow to five

    Keep narrowing until you have the five values that matter most to you right now. This is the hard part, and that's the point.

  3. Save your results

    Save your Top 5 as a PDF or write them down before you close the page. You'll use them in the My Values Snapshot and in this module's journal or class discussion.

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

When two values feel equally important and you have to pick one, the one you keep coming back to is usually the more central value. Trust the pull, not the logic.

How to use your results

Notice what's there

Read each of your Top 5 and sit with it. Does it feel accurate? Does it surprise you? The values that were hardest to choose, or felt uncomfortable to claim, are often the most revealing.

Look for the pattern

Do your five cluster around a theme, like connection, achievement, freedom, or service? The pattern often says more than any single value about what drives you.

Notice the gaps

Is there a part of your life, like school, work, or relationships, where your values aren't being honored? That gap is often the source of a low-level dissatisfaction that's hard to name.

Connect to decisions

Think about a recent choice: a program, a job, a relationship. Does it line up with your values? If it does, it probably feels right even when it's hard. If it doesn't, that friction has a name now.
