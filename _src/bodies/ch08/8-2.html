A good career fit isn't just about choosing something that "sounds cool" or "pays well."

It's about finding work that lines up with who you are: your values, strengths, interests, and how you actually want to live. In this lesson you'll use Career Coach and O*NET to explore options, then choose one career to research in depth with the Career Research Guide.

What career fit actually means

Career fit happens when several parts of your life line up with what a career actually asks of you. You've already gathered most of this in earlier modules. Now you're putting it to work.

Myth-buster

You don't need to pick a career today. Many students start college without a set career, and plenty of people who do have one change direction along the way. This module is about exploring possibilities, not locking in a lifetime decision.

Think less "What job am I going to do forever?" and more "What direction do I want to explore next?"

Think in directions, not job titles

Instead of hunting for the one perfect job, start with a career cluster: a group of related roles that share similar settings, skills, and values. A cluster gives you options and room to change course.

  • Health care
  • Business and finance
  • IT and technology
  • Human services
  • Skilled trades
  • Education

You don't need to choose the job yet, just a direction worth exploring. The tools below help you narrow it from there.

Before you research: start with the life you want

Most career conversations start with "What do you want to do?" A better starting place: what kind of life do you want to build? Financial security, time with people you love, creative work, community impact, where you live, what your mornings look like. The career is the tool. The life is the point.

Your two research tools

Use both. They give you different information that fits together.

Career Coach: start here

OCTC's Career Coach uses regional labor market data and connects careers directly to OCTC programs and local job postings.

  • Regional salary ranges
  • Job outlook: growing, stable, or declining
  • Education and credentials
  • Skills and typical tasks
  • Related OCTC programs
  • Local job postings

Go to Career Coach

O*NET OnLine: go deeper

O*NET OnLine, from the U.S. Department of Labor, describes nearly every occupation in detail at the national level. Use it to go deeper than Career Coach and to double-check what you found.

  • Detailed tasks and work activities
  • Knowledge, skills, and abilities
  • Work context: setting, pace, schedule, people contact
  • Work styles and work values
  • National wages and outlook
  • Related occupations

Go to O*NET OnLine

Not the same as the Interest Profiler. In Module 6 you may have used the O*NET Interest Profiler, which asks what might interest you. O*NET OnLine answers a different question: what is this specific job actually like?

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

Use Career Coach for the local picture: pay, outlook, OCTC programs. Use O*NET for what the work actually requires day to day. For national numbers, the Occupational Outlook Handbook is another good source.

How to research, step by step

  1. Go back to your Career Coach results

    You took the interest assessment in Module 6. Log back in to see your matches. If you want a fresh look, you can retake it anytime.

  2. Look through your top matches

    Open several that catch your attention, including ones that surprise you. For each, check the regional pay, job outlook, education needed, skills, typical tasks, and connected OCTC programs. Which ones feel like a natural fit? Which spark real curiosity?

  3. Choose one career to research in depth

    Pick the one that interests you most, not the one that sounds most impressive. It can be the direction you named in Mile Marker #6, or something new.

  4. Look it up in O*NET OnLine

    Search for the job title. Read the Tasks, Work Context, and Work Styles sections to see what the day-to-day really looks like. Note anything that matches or clashes with what Career Coach showed you.

  5. Fill in the Career Research Guide

    Record what you found, then work through the fit analysis. Save it as a PDF. It goes with your Mile Marker #8 submission.

Using AI in career research

AI Literacy BE Critical BE Responsible

AI can help you think about a career, but it's a poor source for facts about one. Its pay and outlook numbers may be out of date, national rather than regional, or simply wrong.

  • Good uses: "What questions should I ask someone who works as a [career]?" or "What are the hardest parts of working as a [career] that people don't talk about?"
  • Poor uses: asking AI for the salary, outlook, or education requirements and copying the answer into your guide.

Get your numbers and requirements from Career Coach, O*NET, and the Occupational Outlook Handbook. If AI tells you something that doesn't match, trust the source and notice the difference.

Research first. Then decide what it means for you. The Career Research Guide is where you gather the facts, and Mile Marker #8 is where you make sense of them.
