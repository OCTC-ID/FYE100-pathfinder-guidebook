What it measures: the six interest types

Career Coach uses the RIASEC model, a widely used framework developed by psychologist John Holland, to sort interests into six types. Most people have a mix of two or three strong types. Knowing your mix helps you see which work environments and career directions tend to fit people like you.

[[term]] Key Term

RIASEC: six interest types (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) used to match people with work they're likely to enjoy.

  • R

    Realistic

    Doers who like working with their hands, tools, machines, or the outdoors. Practical and results-focused.

  • I

    Investigative

    Thinkers who love working through problems, research, and ideas. Curious and analytical.

  • A

    Artistic

    Creators who value self-expression, imagination, and freedom. Drawn to design, writing, and music.

  • S

    Social

    Helpers who enjoy working with and for people. Drawn to education, healthcare, and community work.

  • E

    Enterprising

    Leaders who like persuading and driving results. Drawn to business, sales, and management.

  • C

    Conventional

    Organizers who thrive with structure and systems. Detail-focused, drawn to data, finance, and process.

Your top two or three types together tell a more useful story than any one type alone. A Social-Investigative mix points somewhere different than a Social-Enterprising one, even though both include Social. Pay attention to the combination, not just the highest score.

How to take the assessment

  1. Open Career Coach and make an account

    Create a free account with your KCTCS email, not a personal one. That saves your results so you can come back to them, in Module 8 and beyond.

  2. Choose the Detailed Assessment

    Career Coach offers a Quick (6 questions), Detailed (30 questions), and Comprehensive (60 questions) version. The Detailed version gives you enough to work with without taking too long. Answer based on what truly interests you, not what sounds practical or what you think you should like. If you're unsure, go with your gut.

  3. Review all of your results

    Look at your interest profile, your top career matches, and the OCTC programs connected to them. Scan past the #1 match to see what patterns show up. Open a few careers that interest you to see pay, education level, and program connections. Then save or download your results for Mile Marker #6.

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

You can retake the assessment anytime. Your interests can shift as you learn more about yourself.

What to do with your results

Results are a starting point, not a verdict. Here's how to use them well.

  • [[tick]]Notice what fits. Does your top type sound like you? Do the matches feel right, surprising, or completely off? Your reaction is information too.
  • [[tick]]Notice what doesn't. If your top match is something you have zero interest in, don't throw out the whole profile. Look at the traits underneath the job title.
  • [[tick]]Connect to programs. Use the OCTC program links in your results to find one or two programs worth exploring. You don't have to commit. Just find what's worth learning more about.
  • [[tick]]Write down your questions. Good results raise questions. What does this program actually require? What's the difference between the diploma and the AAS? Those questions are for your Success Coach.

Using AI to go deeper on your results

AI Literacy BE Responsible BE Reflective

AI can be a useful thinking partner for exploring what your results mean, with one big limit: it doesn't know OCTC's programs, current requirements, or your situation. Use it to think, not to decide.

  • Understanding your profile: "My top interest types are Social, Conventional, and Artistic. What work environments and career directions tend to fit this mix?"
  • Exploring a match: "What does a typical day look like for a [career from your results]? What skills does it usually take?"
  • Finding advisor questions: "Based on my interest in [program or career area], what are five specific questions I should ask my academic advisor before registering for next term?"
  • Thinking through fit: "Help me think through whether [career area] might fit someone who [a short description of your interests and working style]."

Keep it general: don't share your student ID, birth date, or other personal identifying details. And anything AI says about specific courses, sequences, or requirements needs to be checked with your Success Coach or the KCTCS catalog. Use AI to think. Use your advisor to decide.

Your results aren't a map of where you're going. They're a mirror: a reflection of what interests you right now, at this point in your life. That's useful even if it raises more questions than it answers.
