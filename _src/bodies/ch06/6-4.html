An academic plan isn't a rigid schedule. It's a map: a rough picture of where you're headed and what needs to happen, in what order, to get there.

Having one, even a simple, imperfect one, means you walk into your Success Coach appointment with something to react to instead of nothing to start from. This lesson helps you plan strategically, so you're not just picking classes that fit your schedule, but choosing courses that move you toward something.

A plan that changes is still better than no plan. It gives you something to revise.

Think like a strategist, not just a scheduler

Most students treat registration like a puzzle: find classes that don't overlap with work and family. That's necessary, but it isn't enough. Strategic planning asks a few more questions before you open the schedule.

1Know what has to happen first

Most programs have prerequisites, courses you must finish before you can take the next one. In some programs, getting the order wrong costs a whole semester. In others, missing an application or entrance exam deadline can push your start back a full year.

  • Math prerequisites are the most common bottleneck. Many health, science, and business programs need college-level math before you can move ahead. Find out what math your program requires, and when.
  • Application deadlines exist for competitive programs, like many health programs. Missing one can mean waiting a year to start.
  • Entrance exams. Some programs require an entrance exam that takes time to prepare for and schedule. Nursing programs, for example, often use one. Find out now, not after two semesters of prerequisites.
[[term]] Key Term

Prerequisite: a course you must finish before you're allowed to take another course.

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

Ask your Success Coach: "Are there any deadlines, entrance exams, or application windows I need to know about for this program?" That one question can save you a semester or more.

2Be realistic about your load

Full-time is usually 12 credit hours a semester. That doesn't mean 12 is right for you. Students who work full time, care for family, or are coming back after time away often find that a lighter load done well serves them better than a full load done poorly.

  • A rough rule: for every credit hour, expect 2 to 3 hours of work outside class each week. A 12-credit load can mean 24 to 36 hours of studying on top of class time.
  • Your first semesters are for learning how college works for you: what your time really looks like and how demanding your courses are. Starting lighter and doing well is a stronger foundation than overloading and struggling.
  • Part-time is a real option. It takes longer, but finishing at your own pace beats not finishing at all.
[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

Financial aid often requires a minimum number of credit hours. Before you choose a lighter semester, check with Financial Aid about what keeps your aid in place.

3Look more than one semester ahead

You don't need a perfect multi-year plan. But looking past next semester, even roughly, shows whether you're on track, whether your prerequisites will be done in time, and whether your finish date is realistic.

The Academic Planner gives you room for next semester and two more. Start with next semester. Looking ahead, even imperfectly, is the point.

[[tip]] Pathfinder Tip

Not every course is offered every semester. Some are offered only once a year. Missing one can delay you significantly, so ask your advisor which courses have limited offerings.

4Keep it flexible

Life happens. Programs change. You discover you dislike something you thought you'd love, or love something you didn't expect. A good plan absorbs change without falling apart.

  • General education courses often count toward several programs. Taking them early protects you if you change direction.
  • A certificate in one area doesn't lock you out of another program. Stackable credentials let you pivot without starting over.
  • Review your plan with your advisor every semester, not just once.

What strategic planning looks like

The same thinking plays out differently depending on where you're headed.

Interested in nursing

Nursing programs typically have a competitive application with a deadline, prerequisite courses in areas like anatomy and math, and often an entrance exam.

Strategic move: Find out the application timeline now and work backward to plan your prerequisites.

Planning to transfer

Transfer works best when you know your likely university and major, since schools differ in what they accept for a given major.

Strategic move: Name one or two universities you're considering, and ask your advisor which courses fit their requirements.

Exploring a technical program

Many technical programs, like welding, HVAC, and automotive, offer certificates that stack into diplomas and AAS degrees.

Strategic move: Map the full credential ladder so you earn something meaningful at each step, not just at the end.

Still deciding

You're not stuck. General education courses like English, math, and social sciences are required in almost every program and transfer widely.

Strategic move: Start with general education while you explore. It builds a foundation that works wherever you land.

Questions to bring to your Success Coach

Mile Marker #6 asks you to write five of your own, in priority order. These examples get you started. Yours should fit your actual program interest and situation.

  • [[tick]]What are the prerequisites for [program], and are any of them offered only in certain semesters?
  • [[tick]]For [program], are there application deadlines, entrance exams, or competitive admission steps I need to know about now?
  • [[tick]]Based on my plan, what's a realistic timeline for finishing my credential?
  • [[tick]]Which courses could I take early that count toward more than one program, in case I change direction?
  • [[tick]]If I might transfer someday, what should I be doing differently now?
  • [[tick]]What credit load do you recommend for my situation, and how does it affect my financial aid?

Using AI to explore your plan

AI Literacy BE Responsible BE Reflective

AI can help you think through planning questions, but it doesn't know OCTC's programs, current prerequisites, course schedules, or deadlines.

  • Finding questions: "I'm interested in a health program at a community college. What should I ask my advisor about prerequisites and application timelines?"
  • Weighing a decision: "Help me think through the pros and cons of starting with a certificate versus going straight for an AAS in [field]."
  • Understanding ideas: "What does it mean for credits to transfer? What should I ask to make sure mine will?"
  • A rough first draft: "What kinds of courses are usually required in the first year of [general field]?" Then compare it with OCTC's actual program requirements.

Keep personal identifying details out of it. And check every specific requirement, sequence, and deadline with your Success Coach or the KCTCS catalog. When the AI's answer didn't match, notice that. AI gives you a way to think. Your advisor gives you the facts.

You don't need a perfect plan before you talk to your Success Coach. You need enough of one to have a real conversation, where you're reacting to something specific instead of starting from zero.

Your planner, your Career Coach results, and your questions are that starting point. Bring them. The conversation will be better for it.
